Two peptides, two different receptors, one pituitary cell — the forum-famous growth-hormone stack. This page walks through what each one is, why they're combined, what the research actually supports, and what people online report. Claims vs. evidence, side by side.

The short version

A GHRH analog plus a ghrelin-mimetic, paired because together they trigger a bigger GH pulse than either alone. The synergy mechanism is real endocrinology — but the specific combo has never been studied in a trial, neither compound is an approved medicine, and both are banned in tested sport.

01 The two

The signal and the push

The stack pairs a GHRH analog (the signal: "get ready to release GH") with a ghrelin mimetic (the push: "release it now"). Different molecules, different receptors — same pituitary cell.

CJC-1295 · NO DAC / Mod GRF 1-29

  • 29 amino acids — a modified GHRH fragment with 4 substitutions that resist enzymatic breakdown.
  • "No DAC" means no Drug Affinity Complex: unlike the DAC version, it does not bind albumin — half-life is short (~30 min, widely quoted but never directly measured in humans).
  • Result: a brief, pulse-like GH release that mimics the body's natural rhythm, not a days-long "GH bleed."
  • The DAC version is a different compound (6–8 day half-life) — conflating the two is the #1 confusion online.
Plain EnglishThe "get ready" signal. CJC-1295 no DAC knocks on the pituitary's GHRH door and tells it to prepare a pulse of growth hormone — then it's gone within the hour, the way natural signals are.

Ipamorelin · pentapeptide

  • 5 amino acids (Aib-His-D-2-Nal-D-Phe-Lys) — a ghrelin mimetic that hits the GHS receptor.
  • Famous for selectivity: strong GH release with minimal cortisol or prolactin rise — unlike older GHRPs (GHRP-6, GHRP-2).
  • Half-life ~2 hours; notably less hunger stimulation than GHRP-6.
  • Developed by a pharma company (Novo Nordisk), tested in Phase 2 trials — then the program was discontinued.
Plain EnglishThe "go" button. Ipamorelin presses the pituitary's second doorbell — the ghrelin receptor — triggering the actual GH pulse. Its claim to fame is doing this cleanly, without dragging stress hormones along.

Don't confuse the cousins

CJC + DAC

Different drug. Albumin-binding linker → 6–8 day half-life → sustained GH/IGF-1 elevation for over a week ("GH bleed"). The human trials you hear about (Teichman 2006) used this version.

CJC no DAC

This page's compound. No linker → ~30 min half-life → one brief pulse per dose. No human pharmacokinetic trial of this exact molecule exists — the half-life figure is inferred, not measured.

Sermorelin

The older sibling. Another GHRH fragment (actually FDA-approved back in 1997, later discontinued commercially). Similar pulsatile idea, shorter half-life (~10–20 min).

02 Why stack them

1 + 1 = 3

The whole rationale is synergy: GHRH and ghrelin-mimetics hit two different receptors on the same pituitary cell, and the combined GH pulse is bigger than the sum of the parts. This isn't forum lore — it's textbook endocrinology, shown in human pilot data.

The synergy: GHRH primes the cell, the ghrelin-mimetic fires it — together the pulse exceeds the sum.

Why no-DAC specifically?

Pulsatile

The no-DAC version produces discrete pulses that ride the body's natural rhythm — the community's stated reason for preferring it over the DAC "bleed."

Complementary

GHRH analogs alone give a modest pulse; ghrelin-mimetics alone give a modest pulse; together they're synergistic — shown in human pilot data for GHRP+GHRH combinations generally.

The catch

The specific CJC-1295 (no DAC) + ipamorelin blend has never been studied. The synergy principle is established; this exact pairing's effects in humans are forum knowledge, not clinical knowledge.

Plain EnglishTwo doorbells are louder than one. Ringing the pituitary's GHRH doorbell and its ghrelin doorbell at the same time gets a bigger GH shout than either bell alone. The idea is sound — the specific combo just never got its own trial.
03 Proposed mechanisms

Knock, knock — two doors

Both peptides end at the same cell — the pituitary somatotroph — but they knock on different receptors and use different intracellular messengers. The pulse that follows raises GH, which tells the liver to make IGF-1, the downstream mediator of most GH effects.

GH is the messenger; IGF-1 does most of the downstream work — and carries most of the theoretical risk.

CJC's path · GHRH receptor

  • Binds the GHRH receptor on somatotrophs → raises cAMP inside the cell.
  • cAMP primes the cell: more GH synthesized and staged for release.
  • Short half-life → the signal fades within the hour, preserving pulsatility.
Plain EnglishLoading the cannon. CJC tells the pituitary cell to manufacture and line up growth hormone, ready to fire — like cocking a mechanism rather than pulling the trigger.

Ipamorelin's path · ghrelin receptor

  • Binds the ghrelin (GHS) receptor → raises intracellular calcium.
  • Calcium triggers release of the staged GH — the actual pulse.
  • Selective: minimal ACTH/cortisol/prolactin — the "clean pulse" reputation.
Plain EnglishPulling the trigger. Ipamorelin fires the loaded cannon — and unlike older GHRPs, it (mostly) doesn't set off the neighboring alarms for stress hormones.
Why the combo matters mechanistically: cAMP (priming) + calcium (release) converge on the same cell — which is exactly why the combined pulse exceeds either alone. The mechanism is the least controversial part of this page.
04 Honest evidence check

Strong mechanism, thin outcomes

Here's the honest shape of it: real human pharmacology exists (these peptides do raise GH in people), but controlled trials of actual outcomes — body composition, recovery, aging — are essentially absent. Hormone levels were measured; benefits were not.

CJC-1295 Watch which version the data belongs to

CJC-1295 with DAC: human trials existTeichman 2006, Ionescu 2006
Single injections raised IGF-1 1.5–3× baseline for 6–14 days. Real data — for the wrong molecule if you're researching no-DAC.
CJC-1295 no DAC: human trials≈ 0
No human pharmacokinetic or efficacy trial of the no-DAC molecule exists. The "~30 min half-life" is inferred from chemistry, not measured in people.
Program history: abandoned 2006ConjuChem phase II halted
The DAC program's phase II (HIV lipodystrophy) was stopped after a participant death at an Argentine site. Development never resumed.

Ipamorelin Better studied — and a negative trial

Selectivity: GH without cortisol/prolactinRaun 1998 + human pilots
The strongest claim in ipamorelin's file: potent GH release with a cleaner endocrine profile than older GHRPs. Shown in animals and ~40 healthy volunteers.
Phase 2 postoperative ileus trial: missed endpointBeck 2014, n=114, p=0.15
25.3 vs 32.6 hours — not significant. A real, properly blinded, negative trial. Development for that indication stopped.
Human outcome trials (body comp, recovery, sleep)≈ 0
Everything the forums claim — fat loss, muscle, recovery — has no controlled human trial behind it. Mechanism plus anecdotes only.

The stack Status check

FDA

Neither is FDA-approved for any use. CJC-1295's clinical development was abandoned; ipamorelin's pharma program was discontinued. Both circulate as unapproved research chemicals.

WADA

Both are WADA-prohibited (S2: peptide hormones and related substances). Tested athletes, this stack is off-limits — unlike GHK-Cu.

The combo

Zero human trials of CJC-1295 (no DAC) + ipamorelin together. The synergy principle is established science; this specific pairing's effects are entirely anecdotal.

Plain EnglishThe engine is proven; the destination isn't. We know these molecules raise growth hormone in people — that's measured. Whether that translates into the promised body-composition or recovery benefits has never been tested in a proper trial.
05 What people say

Stories, not data

Recurring themes from forums, paraphrased. Every one is an unverified personal story — useful for knowing what to watch for, useless as proof.

Read these like overheard conversations, not results. No controls, no blinding, heavy survivorship bias — and GH-adjacent effects (sleep, water, hunger) are exactly the kind expectation can manufacture.
Unverified anecdote — the pulsing dot marks a paraphrased recurring theme from forums and social media: not verified, not evidence.

Deeper sleep

The most consistent theme: falling asleep faster and waking more refreshed, usually attributed to the nighttime pulse riding the natural GH surge. Often reported within the first week.

Caveat: sleep is the #1 placebo-responsive outcome, and a new bedtime ritual alone improves it. No sleep trial of this stack exists.

Vivid dreams

A close second: unusually vivid, memorable dreams. Frequently mentioned alongside the sleep reports — sometimes as a perk, sometimes as a nuisance.

Caveat: GH-axis activity and dreaming have a plausible link, but dream recall is also boosted by simply paying attention to sleep.

"No hunger spike"

Users switching from GHRP-6 often note much less ravenous hunger on ipamorelin — cited as the reason they chose it over older GHRPs.

Caveat: aligns with ipamorelin's selectivity data, but "less hungry than GHRP-6" is a low bar and still self-reported.

Water retention

Puffy face, swollen fingers, a few pounds of "mystery weight" in the first weeks — the classic GH signature, and the reason some users quit early.

Caveat: genuinely consistent with GH physiology (sodium/water retention) — which makes it one of the more believable anecdote clusters, not proof of benefit.

Tingling hands

Numbness or tingling in fingers/hands, especially overnight — described like mild carpal tunnel. Usually fades if use stops.

Caveat: another textbook GH effect (tissue swelling compressing nerves). Believable as a side-effect report; still uncontrolled.

Faster recovery

Lifters report less soreness and quicker bounce-back between sessions. The claim the whole stack is sold on — and the one with the least evidence.

Caveat: the flagship claim has zero trial support. Better sleep alone (see tile #1) could explain all of it.

"Nothing happened"

The quiet file: weeks of use, no change in sleep, recovery, or anything else — sometimes followed by suspicion the vials were bunk.

Caveat: non-responders and bad product are indistinguishable in anecdotes — which is itself the point about gray-market quality.

The DAC mix-up

A recurring cautionary tale: users buying "CJC-1295" without checking DAC status — then wondering why one version needs frequent use and the other doesn't. Lab-test posts showing mislabeled vials fuel the paranoia.

Caveat: this one's about the market, not the molecule — but it's the most actionable theme here: the label is not the molecule.

Morning freshness

Waking up before the alarm feeling genuinely restored — the morning grogginess just gone. Described as the first thing people notice, sometimes within the first few days.

Caveat: also the outcome most vulnerable to expectation — a new bedtime ritual plus optimism about a new compound both improve mornings on their own.

Skin and nails

Faster-growing nails, skin looking clearer or firmer within a month or two — frequently mentioned by users who weren't even looking for it.

Caveat: skin and nail turnover respond to many things: hydration, sleep, season. GH does influence collagen turnover, but no trial measured cosmetic outcomes for this stack.

Nagging joint eased

Old tendon and joint niggles — elbows, knees, shoulders — feeling smoother and less achy during training. A favorite theme in lifting communities.

Caveat: GH-driven fluid retention in connective tissue could genuinely ease creaky joints short-term — that's symptom relief, not tissue repair, and says nothing about the underlying structure.

Training capacity

Handling more weekly volume and bouncing back between hard sessions faster — usually framed as recovery rather than strength. The work gets easier to repeat.

Caveat: better sleep plus expectation is a complete alternative explanation. No performance trial exists; personal records are not data.

Steadier mood

Feeling calmer and more even-keeled through the day — usually attributed to finally sleeping properly rather than any direct effect.

Caveat: mood tracks sleep quality tightly, so this may be entirely downstream of rest. Self-reported mood is also highly suggestible.

Hair growing faster

Haircuts needed noticeably sooner, hair feeling thicker — usually reported as a neutral-to-positive surprise rather than a goal.

Caveat: growth rate is easy to misjudge and hard to verify. GH/IGF-1 does influence follicles, but faster growth isn't more hair — and nobody measured it.
06 Community protocols

What people discuss, not what's proven

No human trial ever tested this stack, so no official protocol exists. What exists instead is a years-long community conversation about how people structure their use. This chapter maps that conversation's recurring themes — deliberately without amounts, schedules, or administration details. It describes the talk; it is not instructions.

The most-discussed theme in the community: timing use with the night, when the body's own GH pulse runs largest.

Nighttime

The dominant pattern discussed. The overwhelming majority of community reports describe use before bed — the rationale offered is "riding" the large natural GH pulse of deep sleep rather than fighting daytime physiology.

On / off cycling

Breaks are a recurring topic. Many voices argue for cycling periods of use with time off, on the theory that constant stimulation invites desensitization. How long each phase lasts is one of the most argued-about questions — and there is no consensus answer.

Start-low ethos

Veterans preach restraint to newcomers. A near-universal refrain: whatever amounts circulate in forum posts, don't start there. The community's own harm-reduction instinct, such as it is.

Goal-shaped

Sleep seekers talk differently than physique seekers. The conversation splinters by goal — recovery, sleep quality, body composition — which is itself why no single "protocol" ever emerges from the noise.

Why the conversation never converges: no trials to anchor it, huge individual variation, different goals — and a gray market where the vial's contents are themselves uncertain. Ten people can follow ten different approaches and all sincerely report success.

Discussion map, not a protocol. This chapter describes what people talk about online — it does not tell you how much to use, when, how often, or how to prepare or administer anything, and it never will.

07 Safety & unknowns

Raising GH is not free

Growth hormone does a lot of things — that's the appeal and the problem. Every benefit attributed to this stack runs through the same GH/IGF-1 axis that carries the theoretical risks. You don't get one without the other.

IGF-1

Theoretical cancer concern. Chronically elevated IGF-1 is associated in epidemiology with higher risk of several cancers. No trial links this stack to cancer — but the mechanism is the same one being stimulated on purpose.

Metabolic

Insulin resistance. GH opposes insulin; sustained elevation can raise blood sugar and reduce insulin sensitivity. A real consideration for anyone with prediabetes or metabolic issues.

Water & tissue

Edema, joint stiffness, carpal-tunnel-like symptoms. The most commonly reported side effects — GH-driven fluid retention and tissue swelling. Usually reversible when use stops.

Pituitary

Long-term unknowns. What years of repeatedly hammering the somatotroph's two receptors does to pituitary function has never been studied in humans. No data, either direction.

Quality

The gray-market wildcard. Mislabeled DAC/no-DAC vials are a documented problem; unregulated peptides carry contamination and dosing-accuracy risks no trial can account for.

The bottom line on safety: short-term use looks tolerable in the limited human data that exists — but the long-term picture of chronic GH/IGF-1 elevation is genuinely unknown, and the theoretical risks (metabolic, oncologic) are the same pathway as the hoped-for benefits. That's not a scare line; it's the biology.

Plain EnglishThe brain's "release the growth hormone" memo. Your hypothalamus sends this memo to the pituitary several times a day, mostly at night. CJC-1295 is a lab-made copy of that memo, printed on tougher paper.

Stack exampleIpamorelin is a ghrelin mimetic — it hits the same GHS receptor as ghrelin, but engineered for GH release with far less appetite stimulation than natural ghrelin or older GHRPs.

Plain EnglishThe hunger hormone with a side hustle. Ghrelin tells your brain you're hungry and tells your pituitary to pulse GH. Ipamorelin borrows the second job while mostly skipping the first.

The scienceThe pituitary's growth-hormone factory cell. Somatotrophs carry both GHRH receptors and ghrelin (GHS) receptors — which is the entire anatomical reason the CJC + ipamorelin combo can be synergistic: two inputs, one cell.

Stack exampleBoth stack members converge here. CJC knocks via the GHRH receptor (cAMP), ipamorelin via the GHS receptor (calcium) — and the somatotroph answers with a bigger pulse than either input alone.

Plain EnglishThe one cell this whole page is about. Deep in the pituitary sits a cell whose job is making growth hormone. It has two doorbells — and this stack rings both at once.

The scienceThe body releases GH in bursts (pulses), mostly during deep sleep — not as a steady drip. Pulsatility matters: constant GH exposure desensitizes receptors and produces different (often worse) metabolic effects than the same total amount delivered in pulses.

Stack exampleThe no-DAC selling point. CJC-1295 no DAC's short half-life produces discrete pulses like the body's own rhythm — versus the DAC version's days-long "GH bleed," which is widely considered less physiological.

Plain EnglishDrumbeats, not a drone. Natural GH comes in rhythmic thumps, biggest at night. The stack's fans argue short-acting pulses copy that music — while the long-acting DAC version just holds one endless note.

Stack exampleThe critical distinction on this page. CJC-1295 with DAC: 6–8 day half-life, sustained GH/IGF-1. CJC-1295 no DAC: ~30 min, brief pulses. Different compounds, different risk profiles — same name on the vial.

The scienceThe primary stress hormone, released via the HPA axis (ACTH from the pituitary → cortisol from the adrenals). Chronically elevated cortisol breaks down muscle, stores fat centrally, and impairs sleep — the opposite of what GH-stack users want.

Stack exampleThe thing ipamorelin doesn't raise. Older GHRPs bump ACTH/cortisol alongside GH, partly defeating the purpose. Ipamorelin's selectivity for GH over cortisol is a core part of its appeal.

Plain EnglishThe stress hormone that eats your gains. Cortisol breaks muscle down and keeps you wired — exactly what you don't want while chasing GH's build-and-repair effects. A GH peptide that also spikes cortisol is working against itself.

Plain EnglishFeeling better because you did something. A new nightly ritual, closer attention to sleep, the excitement of a new protocol — all manufacture "results" before any molecule does anything. It's not fakery; it's just not the peptide working.

The scienceThe World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List governs tested sport worldwide. GH secretagogues sit in category S2 (peptide hormones, growth factors, and related substances) — banned at all times, in and out of competition.

Stack exampleBoth compounds are WADA-prohibited. Unlike GHK-Cu (not listed), the entire CJC + ipamorelin stack is off-limits for tested athletes — no ambiguity here.

Plain EnglishDrug-testing's banned list — and this stack is on it. Both halves, no gray area. If you compete in tested sport, this page is a museum exhibit, not a menu.

Plain EnglishTried, tested (a bit), never approved. Real drug companies ran real trials on both — and walked away. That history is more informative than any forum thread: the science was interesting enough to fund, not convincing enough to finish.
